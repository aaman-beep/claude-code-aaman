# Offer architecture — how to build the thing you sell

> **STRAWMAN — redline me.** Distilled from one source: a paid-ads operator (~$1M/mo of Meta spend
> for clients) rebuilding a friend's offer live on camera — *"Watch Me LIVE Create a $1M Offer SO
> GOOD, Cold Traffic Begs To Buy"* (youtube.com/watch?v=QgplHio3XJI). Quotes are his unless marked.
> Nothing here is invented; where I've drawn a conclusion he didn't state, it's marked **[inference]**.

**What this is for.** `value_prop_frameworks.md` and `sms_frameworks.md` are *message-line*
formulas — pain → mechanism → proof, at the level of a sentence you send. This is the layer above
them: **what the business actually sells.** You can't route a lever for an offer that doesn't hold
together.

**What this is not.** Not `sms-playbook/offer-matrix.md` (on hold), which sorts *existing* offers
into archetypes to pick a copy lever. Different job, opposite direction.

**How to use it.** Each section ends with **the test** — one question to ask an offer. Eleven
tests. Run them against any offer, yours or a client's; the ones it fails are the work.

---

## A · An offer is three questions, then a derivation

> *"I think people really overcomplicate offer creation. It's literally just who's it for? What's
> the outcome? How do they get it?"*

That's it. Who · outcome · mechanism. Everything else is downstream.

The part people skip is that **deliverables are derived, not chosen**:

> *"this is the person. This is the outcome of the result of the transformation. Given those two
> things, this is what they need."*

So the order is:

1. **Who** — and not just a category. *"That'll break out into a bunch of subniches and personas."*
2. **Outcome** — the transformation, in their unit.
3. **What they need to get there** — list it out, 1–5 items.
4. **Only now: how each one gets delivered.** A 1:1 onboarding call. A group call. A library. A
   training. Done-for-you. Or referred out entirely — *"I might do that on a one-to-one onboarding
   call and I'll give them a 90-day plan… Then you might say, okay, the next problem they're going
   to have is scripting. I don't want to do that personally."*

Your own tolerance for the work is a **legitimate input to step 4 and only step 4**. It doesn't get
to change steps 1–3. If you hate filming, you refer out the filming; you don't quietly drop
"we get you on camera" from the outcome.

> **The test:** can you say who, outcome, and mechanism in three sentences without using the word
> "and"? If the deliverables list came before the outcome, you built it backwards.

## B · The four things a buyer actually prices

> *"they don't actually care about how it happens. They care about things in terms of the value
> equation. What's the outcome? How much work do I have to do? How much risk is there? How long is
> it going to take?"*

Outcome up. Effort down. Risk down. Time down. That's the whole buying calculus, and the ceiling
case is explicit:

> *"if you were like, I'm going to get you leads from content today and you have to do nothing —
> like that'd be the best thing ever."*

Every feature you're proud of either moves one of those four or it's decoration. **[inference]** The
cheapest wins are usually on effort and risk, not outcome — most offers overspend arguing the
outcome and underspend removing the work.

> **The test:** name which of the four your offer is strongest on, and which it's weakest on. If you
> can't name the weak one, you haven't sold it to a stranger yet.

## C · How hard you argue the mechanism depends on how aware the market is

This is the most misread idea in the transcript, so state it precisely. He does **not** say the
mechanism is optional. He says how much of your copy it consumes depends on who's reading:

> *"you kind of only have to go deep into mechanism stuff in terms of your copy if you're hammering
> an audience that's really aware… if you're going into like brick-and-mortar companies or like
> lawyers or dentists, they don't know anything about coming up with viral ideas or any of that.
> You're just selling them the outcome and then you're educating them on all that stuff on the back
> end. If you're trying to sell to like a marketing agency owner, and they're already aware of all
> this content, you're going to have to convince them why your stuff is better."*

So the mechanism's **job** changes; its importance doesn't:

| Market | The mechanism's job | What leads |
|---|---|---|
| **Aware** (they've bought this category before) | **Differentiate** — why yours beats the one they know | The mechanism |
| **Unaware** (they don't know this is possible) | **Realisation** — "wait, I can't do that today? and I *can*?" | The outcome; mechanism does the believing |

The second row is the whole game when it applies, and it's the thing agencies get wrong by
importing aware-market copy into an unaware market. Compare Redo's own finding, arrived at
independently: *"the mechanism's job here is not differentiation — it's realization."*
(`clients/redo/output/2026-06-09-Redo-LayerB-case-development.md`)

His name for exploiting this: sell a **known** mechanism to a market that hasn't met it.

> *"what he's been able to do is go to a super unaware market and just sell them on the mechanism
> of getting content via social media. But he's just figured out a way to sell it to a higher-end
> customer."*

> **The test:** does your market already buy this category? If yes, your mechanism has to beat a
> named alternative. If no, your mechanism has to make them realise something — and leading with
> the result just gets "good for them."

## D · Don't design the offer around your least-qualified client

The sharpest idea in the transcript, and the one with the most expensive failure mode.

> *"You'll almost get this idea of what's required based on your least qualified clients."*

His example: agency owners who add a sales offering because their clients have bad sales teams.

> *"In reality, if one of my clients has a bad sales team, I'm either going to refer them to a sales
> manager I know, or I'll view them as an unqualified client."*

A prospect's missing team, missing time, or missing budget is a **disqualifier**, not a gap for you
to absorb into the deliverables:

> *"Them having a bottleneck of like don't want to have a team, or they don't want to put a certain
> amount of resources behind something. That's not something where you specifically need to go in
> and solve that for them as a part of your offer. That's something that might just tell me they
> might just not be a fit."*

And when they demand you absorb it anyway: *"I don't have any time, I don't have any money to hire
a team, you need to just literally wipe my ass for me as a part of this offer"* → *"I would look
back at them and I would say, good luck getting leads."*

### The loop this creates when you get it wrong

He traces it end to end, and it's worth keeping intact because every step feels reasonable:

> *"the problem with your agency was probably that you weren't charging high enough prices to good
> enough clients. Probably couldn't afford good enough employees, so that when there were fires in
> ops, it would just cause you to have to do it cuz there wasn't anyone that was good enough."*

Then the misdiagnosis: the founder concludes **the model** is broken, pivots, and meets the same
wall in the new model.

> *"That doesn't actually mean that the service is cooked… these are the problems that actually
> cause failure and big pivots and retrospectively looking at the problem in a way that you weren't
> actually looking at the problem. So then you go on to the next business… and that's how you get
> in this loop where you're hitting the same thing over and over again. That's what is most of the
> time causing people multi-year plateaus."*

The counter-move is going *up* market, not down:

> *"rather than going down market and like selling something that's more accessible, you know, they
> include more stuff. They're just going, [no]. The only people that actually buy for big prices
> that I can then run ads to so I can afford the CAC is people up market."*

Because the same fix is worth more up there: *"If you fix content for them, that's like a $3 million
a year problem."*

> **The test:** list the deliverables that exist only because one weak client couldn't do something.
> That's not your offer, that's a disqualification list you accidentally productised.

## E · Price is the lever the rest of the business hangs off

> *"that's why people say pricing is the biggest lever. Like your ability to charge a higher price
> to better businesses is going to unlock the ability to hire better employees, therefore get
> better results, therefore be able to travel."*

The chain: **higher price → better clients → affordable good team → better results → better case
studies → better network → easier next sale.** It runs in reverse just as reliably.

Three practical riders:

**1. The floor creates goodwill below it.** Sell only above a line and everyone under it experiences
you as free value — *"Everyone under that level just feels like you're giving free value… it gets
you tons of goodwill in the market."*

**2. Price in the unit that reads smallest.**
> *"Australians think 4k USD is more expensive than 6k AUD… They just don't even know. Like, you
> think of the average business, they don't even think about what USD is."*
Same money, different number, different answer. **[inference]** This generalises past currency —
per-month vs per-year, per-seat vs per-account, a % vs the dollar figure it resolves to.

**3. Start low deliberately, then climb.**
> *"especially for your first like 10 coaching clients, you can have it at a bit of a lower price
> and just increase it over time. I would be aiming to get it up over time."*

> **The test:** if you doubled the price tomorrow, which clients would leave — and would you
> actually miss them? If the answer is "all of them," the price isn't the problem, the ICP is.

## F · Commit to one offer; the downsell is what lets you say no

Limbo is the expensive state:

> *"being in that limbo state is also where you can lose 6 months for no reason."*
> *"Otherwise, the messaging gets really [messy]… your brand will get a little bit diluted as well."*

His own structure, and note *why* the cheap thing exists:

> *"the way that I'm going to enable myself to be selective with those clients is by having a
> downsell, which is like a coaching thing or a community thing."*

> *"I didn't really want to do group coaching that much… Let me just sell to higher-end clients at
> the agency. I'll have a school group that's like 2K a year. That's just going to be like a
> ridiculous value proposition… Probably could have sold this for 12K. Many people do. I know they
> do. I'm just going to have it at 2K. It's just going to sit in my YouTube description forever…
> It's going to sit on my email list forever. It's going to sit on my IG page forever."*

Two things to copy: the downsell is **underpriced on purpose** (it buys the right to be selective at
the top, it isn't a revenue line), and it's **parked permanently** in every surface rather than
launched.

He also names the cost of not deciding: *"the biggest risk to your business is that you won't want
to do it."* Being honest that you'd earn more on the model you hate is a valid input — *"Are you
okay with saying, I'd probably make more if I just stuck with the agency, and I'm okay with letting
that go because I don't want to do it?"* — but half-committing to both is the one option that
always loses.

> **The test:** if a good-fit prospect can't afford the main offer, what do they buy instead — and
> is it already sitting somewhere public, today?

## G · Archetypes and a play library, not bespoke work

The move that stops the founder being the product:

> *"what I would do with that is I would have archetypes of the types of people that you're
> selling. There's cafes that come in, there's lawyers, there's dentists, there's brokers, there's
> real estate."*

Then a library of plays keyed to each archetype's **real constraint**, not their category:

> *"having a format library of all the different styles of content and basing it on like okay this
> person only has an hour to film every day. So what formats work for that person? Or this person's
> younger, they're hustling… they have time to spend a bit more time on a day in the life video."*

Scope it honestly — this is not a quarter of work:

> *"honestly you could probably do it with dropping a voice note into Claude… That's a one-day
> thing… you don't need to do too many, just the types of people that buy. When more people come
> through you're going to see like oh this guy is a pilot."*

**[inference]** The archetype set is a living artifact — it starts from who already bought and grows
as the odd ones show up. Building it speculatively for personas you've never sold is the failure
mode.

> **The test:** could a competent person who isn't you deliver the first 30 days from the library
> alone? If every new client needs your brain, you have a service, not an offer.

## H · Talk one notch above the market you serve

> *"he's never talking about small businesses. Like, he's talking to small businesses and he might
> say the word small businesses, but his messaging is never like towards broke people. And that's
> the trap of most content people."*

The distinction he draws: *"If you're running a business, learn how to make content cuz it's going
to make your business so much better"* versus *"learn how to build a personal brand so you can sell
a fitness coaching offer in 6 months."* Same audience, different altitude, completely different
client quality.

He's blunt about what sets the altitude by default: *"you get stuck at the low end of the market
because the messaging is all [wrong]. It's all kind of biz-oppy just cuz of how they look as well."*

> **The test:** read your headline as your best client. Does it sound like it was written for
> someone doing a tenth of their revenue?

## I · The offer isn't real until it survives cold traffic

Nothing is proven by warm audiences and referrals. The minimum stack is three things:

> *"it's literally headline, VSL, embedded Typeform or embedded scheduler, whatever your scheduler
> of choice is."*

Don't over-buy the tooling — *"ClickFunnels is too expensive… like $200 a month. Don't do it. I use
it but it's kind of criminal. You can just use Lovable."*

### The VSL skeleton, verbatim-ish

He dictates it live. Roughly six minutes:

1. **Qualifying hook** — *"if you're a small to medium-sized business in Australia, I'm talking type
   A, type B, type C, type D"*
2. **Mission line** — outcome, without the effort, in the time: *"It's my mission to get you leads
   from social media without any ad spend in just a couple of hours a month."*
3. **Proof** — *"What I used to do was blah blah blah, proof proof proof proof proof."*
4. **The discovery moment** — *"Then I walked inside a business… And I saw within one week that this
   business was changed forever."*
5. **The reframe** — *"And I found that really if you're a small business all you need is blank
   blank blank blank."*
6. **The offer** — *"So now what I'm doing is I want to install a content system inside your business
   in the next 90 days to take you from blank to blank."*
7. **How it works** → **book a call.**

And the anti-perfectionism note, which is the actual point: *"that might just be like your first one
that you do but there's no reason that with that outline you can't just do that today. And then you
can literally just do it on your phone."*

**Also build the deck.** *"because you're self-sabotaging sales calls, just make yourself a sales
presentation. It's literally the easiest thing that you can do."*

**And don't skip the humans.** The self-serve funnel is not the whole system:

> *"I got a piece of data from a coaching business that 84% of their sales calls booked from IG
> organic were not self-booked. They were from manual bookings from DMs… So if you're not really
> going hard in the DMs, you're going to leave 80 plus percent of your calls on the table."*

> **The test:** could a stranger who has never heard of you buy this from a six-minute video and a
> booking link? If it needs your reputation to make sense, it's not an offer yet.

## J · The Meta-ads section — where the offer gets proved or killed

The reason this transcript is worth keeping. An offer is a hypothesis; paid traffic is the
experiment. Three variables, in order.

### The three variables

> *"you need to understand, what are the variables? So cost per click, how much does it cost me to
> get someone on the page? Page conversion rate, how many people from the page actually take the
> action?… Then the other variable there is lead quality."*

Benchmarks he gives for a VSL call funnel behind a ~4K offer: **CPC $4–12** · **page CVR 3–8%** ·
**cost per call $200–300**.

### Work backwards from the price

> *"Think like my only purpose in life is to book calls for 250 bucks. That's all I have to do. If
> I do that and I can close and I book 100 sales calls a month, close 25 — you're done."*

Then the correction most people forget:

> *"Then you figure out, oh my show rate's 60%. I actually need to book 140 calls. 140 calls, okay,
> what's that going to cost me on ads? 140 × 250, 35k on ads. So then it's like, okay, I need to
> spend 1,000 bucks a day."*

Revenue target → closes → calls → **show rate** → calls needed → cost per call → daily spend. Run it
before you launch; it tells you whether the price can even carry the traffic.

### Structure

> *"CBO, ad sets grouped by concept. So what's a concept? The angle, the format, the offer, and the
> persona you're talking to."*

**Concept = angle × format × offer × persona.** Inside one ad set: *"I like three hooks, two bodies
inside the ad set."* Launch ~5 concepts at ~$200/day. *"If you were to put CBO, 200 bucks a day
budget, five ad sets, you're off to the races."*

### Diagnosing a failure, in order

> *"you might have an issue where you launch, you've spent your first thousand bucks, no calls. In
> that case, I'm going to look at which of those metrics is off. Is it a cost per click issue, or is
> it a page conversion rate issue?"*

If it's **page conversion rate**:
1. **VSL play rate** → if low, change the headline and the thumbnail (*"we've seen higher play rates
   on Viddlytics"* vs Wistia)
2. **Play rate fine, conversions still bad** → test a whole new VSL, or swap the first 90 seconds
3. **Check ad↔page congruence** — *"If they're not driving enough intent, it's not the right people
   landing on the page. If the page is incongruent with the ads, that's the sort of thing that can
   mess up your conversion rate."*
4. **Check page load** — the click-to-landing-page-view rate catches people who bounce before it
   renders

Timing: swap the VSL after ~**$1–1.5k** of spend if conversion is broken *and* traffic is landing.
Past ~$1.5–2k with low conversions and a clean page, it's the ads.

### Lead quality is always a messaging problem

> *"You run the ads, you've spent 3 grand on ads, you have eight calls booked and six of them are
> unqualified. Nine times out of 10, they'll be coming from one ad… when your lead quality is
> broken, it's usually just that the wrong ads are getting spend."*

The fix is mechanical: **map leads back to ads, find the one, kill it.** *"So let's say one ad's
getting a thousand bucks of spend, it's booked four calls, 250 bucks each but they're all
unqualified. I'm flipping that ad off."* Expect *"like a 10% win rate of your ads if you're good."*

### Two traps that make a working funnel look broken

**1. Meta moves spend off your winner.**
> *"Meta will redistribute spend away from winners at times. So from a media buying standpoint, it's
> very confusing for people cuz they'll have results week one, less results week two, week three,
> they'll look at their whole funnel and think that it's [broken]. In reality… that winning ad
> probably got like a hundred bucks of spend."*
Fix: break the winner into its own campaign, or set a minimum spend on the ad set.

**2. No ad converts alone.**
> *"the way Meta works is it'll show — someone will see like five ads before they convert. So if you
> only have one ad in an ad set, it messes up the sequence… you'll get an ad that might have $300 of
> spend, no conversions. You might think that ad's bad. In reality, it was getting served as the
> first impression, and then another ad might have like 40 bucks of spend, one conversion… In
> reality, the whole funnel of the ads is generating that call."*

His view of how rare this understanding is: *"everyone in lead gen and client getting funnels, most
people are super bad at this. That concept alone, most people don't know that."*

### Organic is not paid-ready

Specificity is the whole difference:

> *"The organic that included specific outcomes and included outcome circumstances, problems — if
> that was in the organic or that was the main point of the organic, it performed way, way outsized.
> Like nothing that we'd launched that was organic that didn't have specific problems, specific
> outcomes that they wanted, performed on pay."*

Fix for a viral piece: re-cut it with a qualifying on-screen hook — *"If you're a small business
looking to scale with content, get leads with content, watch this"* — and a more direct CTA.
*"I wouldn't just launch any random organic."*

> **The test:** what's your target cost per booked call, and does the price support it? If you can't
> answer, you can't tell a bad offer from a bad ad.

## K · Distribution is not monetisation

The premise of the whole conversation: 5 million views in 30 days and nothing to sell.

> *"So, you don't have a funnel, you don't have a website, you don't—" "I got nothing." "What's
> stopping you from building that today?" "I've never done any of this."*

> *"there's no reason that you shouldn't be selling like 50 of these a month already."*

The constraint is almost never the one the founder is staring at. He's grinding on the thing he's
already best at because it's the thing he knows how to grind on. **[inference]** When someone
presents a constraint, check the adjacent number first — the bottleneck is usually one step
downstream of where they're looking.

His related warning against confusing "I'm busy" with "I'm blocked": the operational constraint is
real (*"I didn't have anything to scale it to"*), but it's a reason to fix capacity, not a reason to
skip building the funnel.

> **The test:** if demand doubled tomorrow, what breaks? That's the constraint. It's rarely the
> thing you spent this week on.

## The eleven tests, in one place

| § | The test |
|---|---|
| A | Who, outcome, mechanism in three sentences, no "and"? |
| B | Which of the four (outcome / effort / risk / time) is it weakest on? |
| C | Aware market or unaware — and does the mechanism differentiate or make them realise? |
| D | Which deliverables exist only because of one weak client? |
| E | Double the price — who leaves, and would you miss them? |
| F | What does a good-fit prospect who can't afford it buy instead, today? |
| G | Could someone who isn't you deliver the first 30 days? |
| H | Read the headline as your best client — is it written down to them? |
| I | Could a stranger buy this from six minutes of video and a link? |
| J | What's the target cost per booked call, and does the price support it? |
| K | If demand doubled tomorrow, what breaks? |

**Scoring, when you need one:** run the eleven, mark each **pass / weak / fail**. Fails are the
build order — and they're usually clustered, because a single wrong ICP call breaks D, E, H and J at
once.
